Every path, measured

Wired, wireless and relayed routes to both cores, each measured for throughput and round-trip time rather than assumed. Knowing which path is the slow one is what stops a team from blaming a driver for a network problem. Representative panel — no addresses or device detail.

Collaborative work — my part

In-pipeline sensor register control

Making register writes actually reach the sensor

My contribution: diagnosed why control tags were being silently dropped inside the camera framework, fixed the data-width encoding, and built a property-based side-channel for per-camera targeted register read and write — verified across both cores and every camera. The tempting fix was to write twice and hope; the actual fix was to find out why the first write disappeared.

Wireless connectivity & remote debug

Debugging both cores over the air, reliably

My contribution: drove the wireless connectivity integration on top of a teammate's app — concurrent client and access-point operation, self-healing wireless debug to both cores, and coexistence handling that kept high-throughput transfers from dropping.

02

Low-latency video, streaming & rendering

vendor bring-up · performance
every
frame was: intermittent loss

From dropped and vanished frames to delivering all of them

Multi-sensor camera and display pipelines were intermittently dropping frames — and worse, silently losing them, which is the failure mode you cannot tune your way out of because nothing reports it. Now every frame arrives. Getting there was vendor bring-up in the hardest sense: sensor and display drivers, hardware-triggered capture and frame synchronisation, the composition and display-handoff path, and the buffer and DMA routing underneath — then holding latency and throughput under sustained load rather than in a quiet lab.

How, and why it stayed fixed

Every one of these was root-caused rather than papered over: display-handoff timing failures and composition regressions, DMA misrouting across a dual-SoC boundary, link-recovery faults, and control writes silently dropped inside the camera framework. A retry loop or a longer timeout would have hidden all four and shipped them. Automatic fault recovery exists in the pipeline as a safety net, not as the fix.

Mission Computer systems

Elbit Systems · 2009 — 2011
one box,
every domain defence mission computer

A distributed multi-core mission computer, end to end

Mission computers are where every discipline in embedded arrives at once. I developed a complex distributed application running on custom multi-core embedded platforms that had to integrate video, graphics, analog signal processing, digital signal processing and advanced communication protocols — simultaneously, in hard real time, on hardware built in-house. Getting all of that to coexist deterministically on one platform, without contention, stalls or a dropped deadline, is the whole problem.

And the RTOS underneath it

Acted as the in-house expert on GHS Integrity RTOS, resolving complex OS-level issues and performance bugs across multiple projects — the kind of work where the answer is never a workaround, because there is nowhere for a workaround to hide in a certifiable real-time kernel. Worked throughout in safety- and mission-critical environments across C/C++, Linux, GHS Integrity and VxWorks.

05

Embedded Virtual Avionics (EVA)

BVR Systems / Elbit · 2006 — 2011
worldwide operational with air forces

Trainer aircraft into 5th-generation virtual fighters

EVA puts a complete virtual cockpit onto a basic or advanced trainer aircraft: virtual avionics, sensors and weapons, managed scenarios with user-defined air, land and naval computer-generated forces, hybrid real/virtual environments simulating radar and EW, and fully synchronised replay for post-flight debrief. The solution has been installed in many of the world's leading trainer aircraft and is operational with leading air forces and fighter combat aircraft.

My part

Sole real-time embedded engineer on it. Every embedded board bring-up (Freescale PowerPC, Intel) on GHS Integrity RTOS; the cross-platform, POSIX-compliant C/C++ infrastructure — using ACE and STL — behind the distributed real-time simulation engine; the servers, services and utility libraries; and the low- and mid-level layers that let avionics training systems run on real-time embedded boards. Continued into Elbit on multi-core Mission Computer systems, consulting on GHS Integrity OS-level and performance issues across projects. In a training sortie a missed frame is a mis-taught lesson, so the real-time behaviour was the product.

End-to-end build framework

General Motors · 2019 — 2024
weeks
→ minutes setup time

The problem

Getting a developer or a CI job to a first successful build took weeks. Traditional software development packages and heavy emulators had to be installed and reconciled per target, and every group solved it differently.

before~3 weeks
after~15 min

time to a developer's first successful build · bars log-scaled

What I built

A modular, multi-platform end-to-end build and integration framework with on-demand toolchain downloads in place of monolithic SDPs. It supports native Linux (RedHat, Ubuntu) and cross-compilation from x86_64 to ARM, QNX, RedHat and different Yocto distributions, with defined container images, development workflows, testing, deployment and release pipelines around it. It was adopted by many organisations across GM — numerous software development groups, spanning many projects — and I mentored engineers on build systems, third-party library integration and deployment to embedded platforms. On a safety programme, setup time is how long a fix takes to reach a reproducible, auditable build.
